Essay · No. 01

Nobody asks the willow to stand straight.

On names given from outside, and the old art of not forcing.

4 min read

Somewhere near you there is probably a willow. By a pond, at the edge of a park, leaning over water it doesn’t seem to need. People walk past it every day. Nobody stops to ask why it won’t straighten up.

Its branches fall toward the ground, and no one calls that a problem. It is simply the shape of the tree. We let it lean. We let it trail its fingers in the water. Some of us sit beneath it on purpose, because the space under a willow is one of the few places outdoors that feels like a room.

We extend this courtesy to a tree without a second thought. We rarely extend it to each other.

Consider how the willow got its name. Someone looked at those long, lowered branches and decided the tree was sad. Weeping. The willow never said so. It was growing toward light and water, the way it grows. The sorrow was ours, laid on it from outside, and it stuck.

Then, in the eighteenth century, Carl Linnaeus gave it a formal name, Salix babylonica, after the willows of Psalm 137, where exiles by the rivers of Babylon hung up their harps and wept. Botanists now think those trees were poplars. The weeping willow comes from China; it never grew by those rivers. So the tree carries two names, and both were chosen by someone who wasn’t the tree.

One for a feeling it doesn’t have.
One for a place it has never been.

Some of us know that arrangement well.

The colleague who says you look fine, as if looking were the whole report. The relative who calls it a phase. The doctor who reads the chart faster than the person. The room that gets louder every hour while everyone else seems to hear it at the same volume all day. The explanation that arrives late, makes sense of an entire childhood in one sentence, and is still treated by some as an excuse. The label comes from outside. Given enough time, it starts to sound like the inside.

There is an old idea in Taoist thought called wu wei. It is usually translated as non-action, which makes it sound like doing nothing. It is closer to not forcing. Water doesn’t argue with the stone; it goes around, and over the years the stone is the one that changes shape. The willow doesn’t brace against the wind. It moves with it.

The old texts return to this again and again: what is stiff breaks, and what bends stays alive. We tend to read it as advice about effort. It may be simpler than that. It may just be a description of what happens.

Most of what weighs on us was handed to us. Stand up straight. Make eye contact. Don’t make it a thing. Push through. Be grateful. Be easy to be around. We were taught to give these instructions enormous attention, to monitor ourselves for compliance all day long, and the monitoring becomes a second job that nobody pays for.

So here is a quiet suggestion, and a slightly strange one: you can stop thinking about some of it. Not stop caring. Stop gripping. Set down the thought you were given and notice whether it was ever yours. A great deal of what we were taught to revere turns out, looked at plainly, not to deserve that much of us.

None of this means the hard parts aren’t hard. The willow is soft, and it is also very difficult to end. Cut it back and it grows again. A branch broken off and pushed into wet ground will often root and become a tree of its own. For thousands of years its bark was used to ease pain and fever, long before anyone isolated the compound inside it, salicin, that led the way to aspirin. The thing that looks like grief has been, all along, a source of relief. Both are true. They usually are.

And there is one more thing the willow seems to understand. It owes the wind no explanation. It owes the people walking past no account of why it grows the way it does. The wind, for its part, owes the willow nothing either. This isn’t coldness. It is the end of a debt no one remembers agreeing to. When nobody owes anybody a performance of being fine, or upright, or ordinary, there is finally room for something better than obligation: the kind of care that is chosen.

This is where we will be writing from. About the films and songs everyone is talking about, what they show, and what the reaction to them shows: who leans in, who looks away, and who is quietly relieved to be seen. About conditions that don’t show on the outside, described the way they are actually lived. About the small, ordinary scenes in which someone is asked, once again, to stand up straight.

We won’t be asking anyone to.

Whatever is, already is. The willow has known this for a long time. It is leaning over the water as you read this, exactly as far as it wants to.
